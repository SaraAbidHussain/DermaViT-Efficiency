## Replication of the DermaMNIST CNN vs. ViT Comparison

We replicated the DermaMNIST portion of Amangeldi et al.'s comparison of ResNet18 against Vision Transformer variants, training three of the six model configurations reported in the original paper: the CNN baseline (ResNet18), the paper's designated target model (ViT-Base, patch size 32), and the configuration the authors identify as the best accuracy-efficiency trade-off (ViT-Small, patch size 16). All three were trained on the same 224x224, three-channel version of DermaMNIST described in the paper, using the same augmentation pipeline (horizontal flip, ±10° rotation, mild color jitter) and the same ImageNet normalization statistics.

The original paper does not report the optimizer, learning rate, batch size, or epoch count used in training, stating only that hyperparameters were tuned on ViT-Base and transferred to the other variants. In the absence of this information, we trained all three models under a single fixed configuration — AdamW, learning rate 3e-4 with cosine annealing over 20 epochs, batch size 32 — to keep the three-way comparison internally consistent, and selected each model's test-time weights from the epoch with the best validation accuracy rather than the final epoch, since early experiments showed validation accuracy could decline well before training loss plateaued.

Parameter counts and FLOPs match the paper's reported values almost exactly across all three models, confirming that the architectures themselves were reproduced correctly. Accuracy trails the paper's numbers by a consistent 6.5 to 8.2 points across all three models. Because this gap is similar in size for a small CNN and a much larger transformer alike, it is more likely attributable to our hyperparameter choices than to an error specific to any one model — a single fixed training recipe applied uniformly across models of very different capacity and inductive bias is a plausible source of underperformance relative to a study that tuned each model individually. Training and inference times run consistently higher than the paper's as well, which we attribute to the P100-to-T4 GPU difference between our setup and theirs, alongside per-batch augmentation overhead not itemized in the original methodology.

We examined the ResNet18 result more closely with a per-class breakdown to rule out the possibility that the accuracy gap reflected a fundamentally broken model rather than a training-configuration gap. The majority class in DermaMNIST ("nv," melanocytic nevi) accounts for roughly two-thirds of the test set; a model that always predicted this class would score 66.9%. Our ResNet18 reached 72.2% on a repeated run, with meaningful recall on four of the seven classes (43-52%) — evidence the model is learning class-specific features rather than defaulting to the majority label. The one clear failure is class 6, the rarest class in the dataset at 29 test samples, where the model achieved zero recall. This is consistent with training under plain, unweighted cross-entropy loss on an imbalanced dataset, and is not addressed in the original paper's methodology either.

One finding diverges from the paper's own conclusion rather than just its magnitude. The paper identifies ViT-Small (P16) as the standout trade-off, reporting a real accuracy spread across the three models (80.26 to 81.56) with the smaller ViT variant nearly matching the largest model's accuracy at a fraction of its cost. In our replication, the three models' accuracies cluster within under a point of each other (73.37-74.06%), and ResNet18 — not ViT-Small — comes out ahead on every efficiency metric: fastest inference, smallest FLOPs alongside the smallest parameter count, and the highest efficiency ratio among the three (5.16, against 2.50 for ViT-Small and 0.77 for ViT-Base, computed identically across all three for internal consistency). Where the original study finds a meaningful edge for a particular ViT configuration, our replication under a shared, undifferentiated training recipe finds the CNN baseline to be the more clearly favorable choice, with the ViT variants offering no accuracy advantage large enough to justify their added cost.

Taken together, we consider this a partial replication: the architectural and computational-cost findings (parameter counts, FLOPs) reproduce almost exactly, and the general phenomenon under study — a real accuracy-versus-efficiency trade-off between CNN and ViT architectures — is present in our results as well. The specific ranking the paper arrives at, however, does not hold under our training setup, most plausibly because per-model hyperparameter tuning appears to matter more to a ViT's realized advantage than the paper's methodology section suggests.
