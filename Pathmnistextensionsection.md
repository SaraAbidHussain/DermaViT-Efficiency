## Extension: Does the Trade-off Generalize to PathMNIST?

The replication above raises a natural question: is the specific advantage the paper attributes to ViT-Small (P16) — near-best accuracy at a fraction of the cost of a full-size ViT — a property of DermaMNIST, or of the underlying architectures more generally? To test this, we trained the same three models, under the same training recipe used for DermaMNIST, on PathMNIST, a nine-class colorectal tissue pathology dataset the original paper does not evaluate. Given PathMNIST's much larger size (89,996 training images against DermaMNIST's 7,000), we used a stratified subsample of 14,000 training images, balanced evenly across all nine classes, along with proportionally sized validation and test subsamples — a scope reduction directly analogous to the one the original paper itself applied to Tiny ImageNet for the same reason, time constraints.

The result on PathMNIST looks meaningfully different from our own DermaMNIST replication. On DermaMNIST, all three models landed within a point of each other on accuracy, and the CNN baseline came out ahead on every efficiency measure with no real accuracy cost to differentiate it from the ViT variants. On PathMNIST, ViT-Small (P16) pulls ahead of both other models by a real margin — 92.09%, against 89.79% for ResNet18 and 89.72% for ViT-Base (P32), a gap of roughly 2.3 points — while still training with a quarter of ViT-Base's parameters and matching or beating it on inference speed. This is much closer to the trade-off the original paper describes than anything we observed in our own replication of their tested dataset.

Put plainly: under our training setup, the paper's central finding reproduced more clearly on a dataset it never evaluated than on the dataset it was built around. This is not the tidy confirmation a straightforward generalization test might hope for, but it is an informative result. It suggests that whatever separates the ViT variants from the CNN baseline in this study is not consistently exposed across medical imaging subdomains under a fixed, shared training recipe — dermatoscopic images and tissue pathology slides differ enough in scale, texture, and class structure that the same architectural comparison plays out differently on each. Rather than "does the trade-off generalize," a more accurate framing of what we found is that it generalizes unevenly, and PathMNIST's larger, more class-separable structure may simply be a setting in which a moderately-sized ViT variant has more room to express its advantage than DermaMNIST's small, imbalanced, low-resolution one.

One additional pattern is worth noting rather than treating as a concern: all three models show a substantial gap between validation and test accuracy on PathMNIST — around 7 to 8 points in each case (ResNet18: 98.2% validation versus 89.8% test; ViT-Base: 97.2% versus 89.7%; ViT-Small: 98.9% versus 92.1%). Because this gap is consistent in size across all three architectures, it is unlikely to reflect a problem specific to any one model, and more likely reflects a known property of PathMNIST itself: its test split is drawn from a separate patient and institutional source than its training and validation splits, by original design, specifically to measure generalization beyond the source population rather than performance on held-out samples from the same source. Given this, the accuracy figures above should be read as a genuine cross-institution generalization test rather than a same-distribution holdout evaluation, which is arguably a more demanding and more meaningful comparison than the paper's own DermaMNIST setup allowed for.
